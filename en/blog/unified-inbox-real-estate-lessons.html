Lessons from unifying a real estate agency’s messages

When three channels became one inbox, what actually changed — and what did not go as expected.

These are notes from a real estate project. Numbers that are not yet confirmed with the client are left out — but what we learned along the way applies to any high-volume business.

The starting point

The agency advertised on Divar, talked to customers on WhatsApp and some agents used Bale. Each agent answered on their own phone. Nobody knew how many messages arrived in total, or how many went unanswered.

First discovery: the problem was distribution, not volume

The assumption was that there were simply too many messages. Once every channel landed in one inbox, the real pattern appeared: messages clustered into specific hours, and those were exactly the hours nobody was available. The fix was not hiring — it was covering those windows.

Second discovery: most messages were three repeated questions

Categorising a week of conversations showed that a large share asked only three things: the price, the exact location, and whether the listing was still available. All three were automatable, which freed agents for the serious conversations.

What did not go as expected

We assumed agents would welcome a shared inbox. In week one there was resistance, because it felt like their customers were no longer theirs. The fix was not technical: lead ownership was made explicit in the system and per-agent reporting was added. Adoption followed quickly.

Most automation projects do not fail for technical reasons; they fail because nobody looked at how people actually work.

What we would do differently from day one

  • Spend a week measuring before connecting anything, so the baseline is real.
  • Make lead ownership and reporting explicit in the very first meeting.
  • Teach three daily tasks instead of every feature, and leave the rest for later.

If your business also receives messages across several channels, measure this before choosing any tool: how many messages arrive in a week, how many are answered within fifteen minutes, and how many are never answered at all. Those three numbers usually make the decision obvious.
